# New Jersey UAS Regulatory Summary

## 1. State UAS Regulatory Overview

New Jersey expressly permits federally authorized UAS operation for any purpose subject to federal law and the State's specific prohibitions. P.L. 2017, c.315 criminalizes dangerous or intoxicated operation, correctional-facility activity, first-responder interference, and specified wildlife use, while preempting only county or municipal private-use rules that are inconsistent with the act. The law does not create a separate state UAS registration or general commercial permit, and its critical-infrastructure provision supplies an application pathway rather than an automatic state buffer. State Parks, NJDEP contracts, federally funded NJDOT work, aerial pesticide application, and Rowan University add mission-, funding-, or property-specific controls.

## 2. Statewide UAS Laws and Regulations

### NJ-001 — State UAS operating authority, critical-infrastructure application pathway, and inconsistent-local-law preemption

**Authority:** N.J. Stat. §§ 2C:40-27, 2C:40-29, 2C:40-30; P.L. 2017, c.315  
**Status:** Current / in force

New Jersey permits a person authorized by federal law to operate a UAS in the State for any purpose when the operation complies with applicable federal law and the State's specific prohibitions. The statute does not create a separate state registration system or general commercial-flight permit. A critical-infrastructure owner may pursue a federal restriction under the cited FAA process, but this provision does not itself create a fixed state buffer. New Jersey also preempts county or municipal enactments concerning private UAS use only when they are inconsistent with P.L. 2017, c.315. That qualified language should not be treated as invalidating every local property, launch, landing, or generally applicable rule.

### NJ-002 — Dangerous or intoxicated UAS operation and restraining-order compliance

**Authority:** N.J. Stat. § 2C:40-28(a), (e)-(g); P.L. 2017, c.315  
**Status:** Current / in force

A person commits a disorderly persons offense by knowingly or intentionally operating a UAS in a manner that endangers another's life or property; a court must consider federal small-UAS safe-operation standards when deciding that issue. Operating a UAS while under the influence of specified intoxicants or with a blood-alcohol concentration of 0.08 percent or more is also a disorderly persons offense. A person subject to a restraining order violates that order by knowingly flying within a prohibited distance of the protected person or location. A conviction under the UAS section does not merge with listed harassment, stalking, privacy, obstruction, contraband, contempt, or other criminal convictions arising from the conduct.

### NJ-003 — Correctional-facility and first-responder UAS offenses

**Authority:** N.J. Stat. § 2C:40-28(b)-(c); P.L. 2017, c.315  
**Status:** Current / in force

New Jersey makes it a fourth-degree crime to knowingly or intentionally create or maintain a condition endangering a correctional facility's safety or security by operating a UAS on the premises or in close proximity without license or privilege. Knowingly using a UAS to surveil or gather information about a correctional facility without license or privilege is a third-degree crime. The definition covers jails, prisons, lockups, penitentiaries, reformatories, training schools, and similar New Jersey facilities. Knowingly or intentionally operating in a manner that interferes with a first responder actively responding or engaged in air, water, vehicular, ground, or specialized transport is separately a fourth-degree crime.

### NJ-004 — UAS use to take wildlife or interfere with lawful taking

**Authority:** N.J. Stat. §§ 2C:40-28(d), 23:7A-2(i); P.L. 2017, c.315  
**Status:** Current / in force

New Jersey makes it a disorderly persons offense to knowingly operate or use a UAS to take or assist in taking wildlife. It also adds UAS operation to the conduct prohibited when done for the purpose of hindering or preventing another person's lawful taking of wildlife. The latter interference provision contains exceptions for law-enforcement and conservation officers enforcing law and for a private landowner or agent on that owner's land or water. Unrelated environmental, engineering, or mapping activity is not automatically a wildlife-taking offense, but flight behavior, project purpose, wildlife-location deliverables, and interaction with lawful hunters should be assessed and documented before operating in active habitat or hunting areas.

### NJ-008 — Commercial aerial-pesticide applicator licensing and operating restrictions

**Authority:** N.J.A.C. 7:30-6, 7:30-7, 7:30-9, 7:30-10.6  
**Status:** Current / in force

New Jersey treats aerial pesticide application as a specialized licensed activity. A commercial applicator must complete the core and Category 11 aerial requirements; on-the-job training is mandatory for Category 11, and an ordinary pesticide operator may not perform aerial applications. A business offering pesticide application for hire must also hold a pesticide applicator business license and the required insurance. N.J.A.C. 7:30-10.6 adds aircraft-application controls, including occupied-site buffers, road-right-of-way limits, written consent and retained records for deposition near private residences, and restrictions on non-agricultural broad-spectrum applications. A drone used to dispense pesticide changes the mission from imagery collection to regulated aerial application; product-label, notification, spill, and record rules remain additional requirements.

## 3. State Agency UAS Requirements

### NJ-005 — Unmanned Aerial Vehicles Policy 2.38 and special-use-permit pathway

**Authority:** State Park Service Policy 2.38; N.J.A.C. 7:2-1.4(b)  
**Status:** Current agency policy

State Park Service Policy 2.38 prohibits UAV operation on all lands and waters it administers unless specifically approved by the Assistant Director under N.J.A.C. 7:2-1.4(b). Requests are evaluated for federal and state compliance, visitor and resource safety, historic and natural-resource impacts, emergency operations, public services, and conflicts with existing uses. When a request is compatible, the Service may issue a special-use permit identifying the operating area and conditions. The published policy describes pathways for scientific research, official use, emergencies, and commercial activity; site-specific materials may add stricter terms. Liberty State Park, for example, requires a policy-waiver request, SUP, insurance, Part 107 certificate, registration, flight plan, notices, and fees.

### NJ-006 — Small UAS Flight Operations Manual requirements for contractors and outside data

**Authority:** NJDEP sUAS Flight Operations Manual §§ 4.4, 5.1 and related flight provisions  
**Status:** Current agency manual

Any NJDEP contract or project using drone data collected by someone other than a current Department pilot must be coordinated with the Division of Information Technology before the contract is signed or the flight occurs. Contracted flights require DOIT approval and must follow the Department's SOP and Flight Operations Manual. The manual applies cybersecurity practices to contracted pilots, including removable media, non-networked transfer, no direct internet streaming, controller isolation, dedicated tablets, and limited data sharing. It also establishes crew, planning, site-assessment, privacy, documentation, logging, and reporting controls. Separately offered outside data requires pilot credentials, aircraft registration, applicable authorizations and property permission, privacy editing, and available flight-plan information before Department use.

### NJ-007 — Restriction on covered foreign UAS for federally funded NJDOT work

**Authority:** NJDOT Quality Improvement Advisory No. 066 (Dec. 19, 2025)  
**Status:** Current agency funding and contract directive

NJDOT Quality Improvement Advisory 066 directs staff, contractors, and consultants working on FHWA-funded projects or grants to implement the American Security Drone Act and OMB Memorandum M-26-02 beginning December 22, 2025. Pending orders for covered UAS may not be paid with federal funds, and covered systems already in a fleet may not be operated, maintained, or used for data processing on the federally funded project or grant. NJDOT describes covered UAS as including aircraft, flight controllers, cameras, and ground-control stations supplied by manufacturers based in or controlled by a foreign-adversary country, and names DJI and Autel as examples. Physical quarantine is recommended to prevent accidental use.

### NJ-009 — Unmanned Aerial System (Drone) Policy

**Authority:** Rowan University Senate Resolution 190201-1  
**Status:** Interim university policy listed by the University

Rowan's published UAS policy applies to vendors and unaffiliated persons operating on University property. Indoor and outdoor uses must be reviewed and approved by the applicable dean, department chair or head, and Public Safety through the University's permission-request process. Outdoor University research, teaching, or operational flights require advance written departmental and Public Safety permission, and Public Safety must be notified before operation. The policy bars operation over an in-use University sporting facility or event without advance written permission and restricts camera-equipped drones around University housing. It also prohibits harassment and requires applicable federal, state, and municipal compliance. Permission is discretionary and may cover one-time or recurring operations.

## 4. Non-Regulatory Context

New Jersey maintains an active NJDOT UAS program and regularly considers new drone legislation. Bills introduced in 2025 and 2026 concerning airports, military activity, schools, weaponized drones, utility inspections, and critical infrastructure were reviewed as proposals only; none was treated as enacted without a chapter-law or current-codification basis. The State's 2024 public concern about unidentified aircraft likewise did not itself create a lasting general commercial-flight restriction. Future updates should check those subjects because the legislative environment remains active.

## 5. Unresolved Operational Questions

1. **Local-rule consistency:** Does a county or municipal rule govern private UAS use, and is it inconsistent with P.L. 2017, c.315 or instead a property, launch, landing, or generally applicable rule outside the narrow preemption clause?
2. **Correctional proximity:** Is the site on or close to any broadly defined correctional facility, and what written license or privilege supports the planned geometry and data collection?
3. **Emergency operations:** Are first responders active or likely to become active at or near the site, and what stop-work or deconfliction process protects their air, water, vehicle, ground, and specialized transport?
4. **State property:** Does the mission use State Park Service or Rowan property, and have current approvals, insurance, operating areas, no-drone zones, notices, and lead times been confirmed?
5. **Agency-client controls:** For NJDEP work, has DOIT coordinated and approved the contractor before contract execution or flight, and can the proposed crew, hardware, data path, privacy process, and documentation satisfy the manual?
6. **Funding and equipment:** Is any NJDOT assignment federally funded, and does every aircraft, controller, camera, ground station, data-processing tool, manufacturer, and pending purchase satisfy current covered-UAS restrictions?
7. **Special payloads:** Does the aircraft dispense pesticide, and if so are Category 11 certification, business licensing, insurance, consent, buffer, notification, recordkeeping, and product-label requirements resolved?
