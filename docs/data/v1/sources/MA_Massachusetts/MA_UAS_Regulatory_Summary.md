## 1. State UAS Regulatory Overview

Massachusetts is notable for **not** having enacted a comprehensive statewide UAS statute. There is no Massachusetts equivalent of Maryland's express state-preemption provision, no enacted state law governing law-enforcement UAS surveillance, and — contrary to what several secondary drone-law websites report — no currently enacted state statute banning weaponized drones. The state's actual UAS-relevant authorities are narrower and more scattered: the Department of Conservation and Recreation (DCR) requires a Special Use Permit before a UAS may take off or land on DCR-managed land (302 CMR 12.04(28)(g)), applying a general aircraft-landing rule that does not itself mention drones or UAS; MassDOT's Aeronautics Division has operated an internal UAS policy since 2017 governing MassDOT/MBTA employees and contractors (not private operators generally); and a generic "hunting from an aircraft" prohibition (M.G.L. c. 131, § 65) is widely reported, though not independently confirmed at the regulatory level in this pass, to be applied by MassWildlife to drone-assisted hunting. In the absence of a state preemption statute, the leading authority on the state/local/federal boundary is *Singer v. City of Newton* (D. Mass. 2017), a federal district-court decision that struck down four provisions of a municipal drone ordinance on conflict-preemption grounds — but this leaves the door open for Massachusetts municipalities to enact their own ordinances subject only to case-by-case federal preemption challenges, unlike states with an express state-law preemption provision. Massachusetts's land-surveying licensing statute (M.G.L. c. 112, § 81D) uses only generic "in the air" language, with no UAS-specific Board guidance located. Substantial UAS/robotics legislation remains pending: most notably H.5444 ("Responsible Robotics Act"), which would ban weaponized drones/robots and impose law-enforcement warrant requirements, passed the House 155-1 in May 2026 but had not passed the Senate or been signed as of this research date; five additional bills addressing critical-facility no-fly zones, school overflight, broader take-off/landing restrictions, and a Chinese-drone sales ban remain pending in committee. No Massachusetts AG opinion, operative executive order, or state UAS procurement/country-of-origin restriction was located.

## 2. Statewide UAS Laws and Regulations

### Hunting from Motor Vehicle, Snowmobile, Aircraft or Watercraft — M.G.L. c. 131, § 65
*Binding law | Current; drone-specific application not independently verified in this pass*

**Objective Summary:** M.G.L. c. 131, § 65 makes it unlawful, except as otherwise provided by law, to "hunt any bird or mammal by the aid or use of any motor vehicle, including a snowmobile, helicopter or other aircraft." The statute does not define "aircraft" or separately address unmanned aircraft. Multiple secondary drone-law compilations describe MassWildlife as applying this prohibition to drones — barring use of a UAS to locate, drive, or take game — but this research pass could not independently open and verify a specific 321 CMR provision or MassWildlife-published guide page stating a UAS-specific rule.

### Singer v. City of Newton — Municipal UAS Ordinance Preemption
*Court decision (federal, D. Mass.) | Current; not appealed per sources reviewed*

**Objective Summary:** In *Singer v. City of Newton*, No. 1:17-cv-10071-WGY (D. Mass. Sept. 21, 2017), an FAA-certificated recreational drone pilot challenged four provisions of a December 2016 City of Newton ordinance: a local UAS-registration requirement and three flight-operation restrictions (a below-400-foot altitude ceiling over private property absent consent, a ban on flight over public property without permission, and a beyond-visual-line-of-sight restriction). Judge William G. Young held these four provisions were conflict-preempted because, in combination, they obstructed federal objectives and directly conflicted with FAA regulation of low-altitude navigable airspace; the court rejected a field-preemption theory but found conflict preemption as to all four challenged provisions. Unchallenged parts of the ordinance were left standing.

> **Preemption-posture note:** Unlike Maryland (which has an express statutory preemption provision, Md. Code Ann., Econ. Dev. § 14-301), Massachusetts has enacted no state statute addressing whether counties or municipalities may regulate UAS. *Singer* rests entirely on federal conflict-preemption doctrine rather than any state preemption statute — meaning a Massachusetts municipality's drone ordinance is vulnerable to a federal challenge on a provision-by-provision basis, but nothing in Massachusetts law itself forecloses a municipality from enacting one. AEC firms should still expect to encounter, and should independently check for, local drone ordinances in each Massachusetts municipality where they plan to operate (local ordinances are deferred from this research phase).

## 3. State Agency and Facility-Specific UAS Requirements

### Rules of Conduct on DCR Properties — Special Use Permit for Aircraft (including UAS) Takeoff and Landing — 302 CMR 12.04(28)(g)
*Binding regulation | Current; DCR was engaged in active rulemaking on this chapter as of this research date*

**Objective Summary:** 302 CMR 12.04(28) provides that, unless authorized by a Special Use Permit issued under 302 CMR 12.17(2), no person may, except in an emergency, "bring, take off, land or cause to descend on DCR property any airplane, helicopter, sea plane, so-called ultra-light aircraft, or any other apparatus." The rule does not separately define or use the words "unmanned aircraft," "UAS," or "drone." DCR's practice, reflected in third-party compilations, applies this general aircraft-landing provision to UAS takeoff and landing on the roughly 450,000 acres of state forests, beaches, reservations, parkways, and waterways it manages. DCR's Office of General Counsel listed 302 CMR 12 among regulations "Under Review," with a draft revision and a 2026 public-hearing notice posted as of this research date.

### Interim (Internal) Policy for the Use of Unmanned Aircraft Systems (UAS) — MassDOT Aeronautics Division
*Official agency policy | Current (issued September 6, 2017; still labeled "Interim")*

**Objective Summary:** MassDOT's Interim Policy for the Use of UAS centralizes UAS authority under the Aeronautics Division ("MassDOT Aeronautics") for all MassDOT and MBTA divisions. No employee, contractor, or agent may use a UAS for a MassDOT or MBTA purpose without prior notification to, and compliance confirmation by, MassDOT Aeronautics. The policy prohibits employee-owned UAS for MassDOT purposes, requires FAA-compliant licensing and waivers, restricts collection of "personally identified UAS data," requires flight logging, and requires advance MassDOT Aeronautics approval of any UAS procurement contract or subcontract. This is an internal state-agency policy, not a requirement imposed on private commercial UAS operators generally.

### Professional Licensing — Massachusetts Land Surveying Statute (M.G.L. c. 112, § 81D)
*Binding general regulation | No UAS-specific Board guidance located*

**Objective Summary:** M.G.L. c. 112, § 81D defines "practice of land surveying" to include measuring and locating lines, angles, elevations, and natural or manmade features "in the air, on the surface of the earth, within underground workings, and on the beds of bodies of water," for purposes including determining boundaries, areas, and volumes, and preparing maps and property descriptions. Unlike Maryland's analogous statute, Massachusetts's definition does not separately name "aerial photogrammetry" or any other aerial-imaging or remote-sensing method. No standalone Board of Registration of Professional Engineers and Land Surveyors rule, advisory ruling, or FAQ specifically addressing UAS-derived photogrammetric or LiDAR survey deliverables was located during this research pass.

## 5. Unresolved Operational Questions

- **No state preemption statute:** Massachusetts has not enacted a state law addressing whether municipalities may regulate UAS. *Singer v. City of Newton* provides persuasive federal conflict-preemption authority against certain categories of local restriction (registration, altitude ceilings, blanket public-property bans, BVLOS restrictions) but does not eliminate the risk that a given Massachusetts municipality maintains its own drone ordinance. AEC firms should check for local ordinances in each municipality where they plan to operate — a step this research phase intentionally defers but that remains operationally necessary.
- **DCR's active rulemaking:** 302 CMR 12.00 (Parks and Recreation Rules), which DCR applies as its basis for requiring a Special Use Permit before UAS takeoff/landing on DCR land, was listed as "Under Review" with a draft revision and a 2026 public-hearing notice as of this research date. The drone-related Special Use Permit requirement could change; confirm current text and any park-specific conditions directly with DCR before a specific engagement.
- **Hunting-related drone restriction not independently confirmed at the regulatory level:** Widely reported MassWildlife drone-hunting rules (a bar on using a drone to locate, drive, or take game) could not be independently verified against a specific 321 CMR provision or MassWildlife's own published guide in this pass. Confirm directly with MassWildlife before relying on these parameters for a land-management or forestry UAS survey near active hunting areas.
- **Pending "Responsible Robotics Act" (H.5444) and companion bills:** H.5444 passed the House 155-1 in May 2026 and remained pending in the Senate Committee on Ways and Means as of this research date; Massachusetts's 2025-2026 formal legislative session was scheduled to conclude July 31, 2026. Whether H.5444 or the five companion Transportation Committee bills (S.2454, H.3618, H.3663, H.3749, S.2438, H.3800) advanced further, died, or moved to informal-session status after that date was not confirmed in this pass and should be rechecked before relying on any pending-bill status stated in this report.
- **Land-surveying licensure and UAS-derived deliverables:** No Board rule or guidance specifically addressing UAS-derived (as opposed to manned-aircraft) photogrammetric or LiDAR survey products was located, and Massachusetts's statutory "in the air" language is less textually direct than Maryland's explicit "aerial photogrammetry" reference. Confirm directly with the Board of Registration of Professional Engineers and Land Surveyors before relying on this citation for a specific project's licensure determination.
