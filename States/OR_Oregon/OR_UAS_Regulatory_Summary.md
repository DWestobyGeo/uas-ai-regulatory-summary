## 1. State UAS Regulatory Overview

Oregon consolidates its UAS law in a single statutory chapter (ORS 837.300–837.998) and vests UAS regulatory authority solely in the legislature, preempting most local operational ordinances except for park takeoff/landing rules. The newest development affecting AEC-adjacent flying is the 2026-effective OPRD rule restricting drone takeoff/landing on state park and ocean-shore property to designated areas. Oregon also has a tiered interference statute (ORS 837.374) with felony-level exposure for interference with wildfire suppression or emergency response.

## 2. Statewide UAS Laws and Regulations

### ORS 837.372 — Operation over critical infrastructure facility; ORS 837.370 — Operation over privately owned premises; ORS 837.385/.387 — Preemption
*Binding law | Current*

**Objective Summary:** ORS 837.372 makes it a Class A violation to fly at/below 400 ft AGL over a fenced/posted critical infrastructure facility (power plants, substations, water/wastewater facilities, telecom switching offices, ports, rail yards, high-hazard dams, correctional facilities) absent the facility owner's consent, ownership/occupancy, or FAA-authorized commercial operation (837.372(3)(i)). ORS 837.370 prohibits operating a drone over private property in a manner that intentionally, knowingly, or recklessly harasses or annoys the owner/occupant. ORS 837.385 vests UAS regulatory authority solely in the legislature, preempting local operational ordinances except for park takeoff/landing rules under ORS 837.387 (which must still allow utility-provider line-inspection access and public-body emergency use). ORS 837.360/.362 impose public-body UAS registration/data-policy requirements (not applicable to private operators). ORS 837.365 prohibits weaponized UAS.

**Practical Interpretation**
- *AEC Industry UAS Expert Perspective:* Rely on the 837.372(3)(i) commercial/FAA exemption for critical-infrastructure inspection work, but get written facility-owner consent when it's easy to obtain — it removes any field ambiguity. Because local ordinances are narrowly limited to park takeoff/landing, the main site-specific check for most projects is simply whether the launch/land point is inside a park.
- *AEC Industry Legal Counsel Perspective:* Keep FAA authorization and commercial-purpose documentation on file for any critical-infrastructure flight, since establishing the exemption is the operator's burden if challenged. For private-property work, document flight altitude and duration to rebut any claim of intentional or reckless harassment under 837.370.

### ORS 837.374 — Interference with aircraft, wildfire suppression, law enforcement, or emergency response
*Binding law | Amended 2023*

**Objective Summary:** Tiered penalty structure: reckless conduct is a Class A violation; knowing/intentional conduct is a Class A misdemeanor (up to 1 year jail, $6,250 fine); conduct causing death or serious physical injury is a Class A felony (up to 20 years). Covers directing a laser at an aircraft, crashing into an aircraft, preventing aircraft takeoff/landing, or interfering with wildfire suppression, law enforcement, or emergency response operations. A 2025 bill to further elevate penalties (HB 3426) passed the House but died in the Senate at sine die on June 27, 2025 — it is not current law.

**Practical Interpretation**
- *AEC Industry UAS Expert Perspective:* Build active-incident awareness into wildfire-season scheduling — check for TFRs and coordinate with incident command before flying anywhere near a fire, law-enforcement operation, or emergency response, since "interference" is broadly defined and doesn't require actual contact with another aircraft.
- *AEC Industry Legal Counsel Perspective:* Document pre-flight airspace/TFR checks for any job scheduled during fire season, and train field crews that felony-level exposure attaches even without intent to interfere if the conduct is reckless and causes serious injury — this warrants a written pre-flight checklist item, not just situational judgment.

## 3. State Agency UAS Requirements

### Aviation — Oregon Department of Aviation (ODA)
*Binding administrative regulation | Public-body only*

**Objective Summary:** OAR 738-080-0045 requires Oregon public bodies to register each UAS with ODA at $25 (under 55 lbs) or $50 (55 lbs+). Private/commercial operators are not required to register with ODA.

**Practical Interpretation**
- *AEC Industry UAS Expert Perspective:* No action needed for a private fleet; only relevant if the firm operates equipment issued or registered under a public agency's own UAS program.
- *AEC Industry Legal Counsel Perspective:* Confirm registration status in writing if flying agency-owned equipment under a public contract — noncompliance would be the agency's exposure, but could still affect project continuity.

### Parks and Public Lands — Oregon Parks and Recreation Department (OPRD)
*Binding administrative regulation | New, effective May 1, 2026*

**Objective Summary:** Drone takeoff/landing is prohibited on all OPRD property and the entire public ocean shore except within designated UAS Operation Areas; a permit pathway remains available for commercial/research use outside those areas. First areas opened without a permit requirement beginning August 8, 2026; more sites to be added over time. Overnight-use facilities are categorically closed to UAS takeoff/landing.

**Practical Interpretation**
- *AEC Industry UAS Expert Perspective:* Check the current OPRD UAS Operation Areas list before scoping any coastal, dune/erosion, or beach-access project, and build permit-application lead time into the schedule if the site falls outside a designated area — site plans made before mid-2026 need re-checking against this rule.
- *AEC Industry Legal Counsel Perspective:* Retain the permit approval (or documentation that the site is within a designated Operation Area) in the project file before mobilizing a crew; treat overnight-use facility sites as an absolute exclusion with no permit workaround.

### Professional Licensing — Oregon State Board of Examiners for Engineering and Land Surveying (OSBEELS)
*Advisory guidance issued by licensing board*

**Objective Summary:** OSBEELS, which regulates the practice of land surveying and photogrammetric mapping under ORS Ch. 672, has published a brochure specifically addressing UAS use in surveying/photogrammetric mapping, confirming UAS as a recognized tool for licensed practice.

**Practical Interpretation**
- *AEC Industry UAS Expert Perspective:* Pull the current OSBEELS brochure directly and use it to set the internal QA/QC workflow for which drone-derived deliverables require registered-professional sign-off.
- *AEC Industry Legal Counsel Perspective:* Board-issued practice guidance is a strong indicator of how OSBEELS would evaluate a complaint, even though nonbinding — cite it in internal compliance documentation, but confirm current content directly with the Board before relying on specific thresholds in a client contract.

### Procurement / Equipment Restrictions
*Category reviewed — no Oregon-specific state law found*

**Objective Summary:** No Oregon state law or executive order specifically restricting government UAS purchase/use by manufacturer or country of origin was located. Secondary reporting (DroneXL, March 2026) indicates Oregon public-safety agencies have been significantly affected by the federal FCC "Covered List" ban on new foreign-made drone models/components.

**Practical Interpretation**
- *AEC Industry UAS Expert Perspective:* No equipment restriction currently limits fleet choice for private commercial work; public-safety-agency clients may be operating a reduced or aging fleet due to the federal covered-list disruption, which could create contract opportunities for compliant-equipment support work.
- *AEC Industry Legal Counsel Perspective:* No state compliance obligation to build into contracts for private work; if contracting with an Oregon public-safety agency, confirm whether federal covered-list compliance is being passed through as a contract term.
