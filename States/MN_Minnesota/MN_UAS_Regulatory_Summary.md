## 1. State UAS Regulatory Overview

Minnesota regulates UAS through a combination of criminal statutes, a distinct state aircraft-registration and commercial-licensing regime administered by the Minnesota Department of Transportation (MnDOT) Office of Aeronautics, and agency-specific rules and policies from the Department of Natural Resources (DNR) and public universities. Unlike some states, Minnesota has not enacted a statewide statute preempting local UAS ordinances, so municipalities, counties, and public entities such as the University of Minnesota remain free to — and in a number of documented cases do — impose their own additional UAS restrictions; MnDOT itself maintains a running (non-exhaustive) list of known local ordinances. At the state level, Minnesota's most distinctive AEC-relevant feature is that commercial UAS operation is subject to a state aircraft-registration requirement (Minn. Stat. § 360.55, Subd. 9-10) and, per MnDOT's own guidance, a separate state Commercial Operations License for operators whose flight-derived work product is provided to a third party — a licensing regime broader than the FAA Part 107 baseline that most AEC firms will need to affirmatively address before flying commercially in the state. Substantive UAS restrictions exist for correctional facilities (Minn. Stat. § 243.552), law enforcement use (Minn. Stat. § 626.19), DNR-managed state parks/recreation areas/waysides (Minn. R. 6100.2200 and DNR policy), hunting-related equipment use (Minn. Stat. §§ 97B.081, .085, .086, as applied to drones by DNR's own enforcement guidance), and university property (University of Minnesota administrative policy). No Minnesota-specific UAS procurement, manufacturer, or country-of-origin restriction, executive order, attorney general opinion, or state appellate court decision was located in this research pass.

## 2. Statewide UAS Laws and Regulations

### Minn. Stat. § 626.19 (2025), enacted 2020 c 82 s 5, amended 2025 c 35 art 5 s 23 — Use of Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (Law Enforcement)
*Binding law or regulation | Current / in force*

**Objective Summary:** Minn. Stat. § 626.19 requires a law enforcement agency to obtain a search warrant before using a UAV, subject to 11 enumerated exceptions (Subd. 3) including emergencies, imminent risk of evidence destruction, heightened-risk public events, terrorist-attack countermeasures, disaster response, threat assessment, reasonable-suspicion public-area monitoring, crash reconstruction, training/public relations with consent, government-entity requests, and active missing-person searches. Subd. 4 bars deploying facial recognition/biometric matching without a warrant, bars equipping a UAV with weapons, and restricts collecting data on protests/demonstrations absent a warrant or exception. The statute also sets documentation (Subd. 5), data classification/retention (Subd. 6, generally private/nonpublic data, deletion within 7 days absent an active investigation), an exclusionary rule for violations (Subd. 7), a civil remedy (Subd. 8), public-comment and written-policy requirements (Subd. 9-10), sealed-warrant notice procedures (Subd. 11), and annual reporting to the commissioner of public safety and the state court administrator (Subd. 12).

**Practical Interpretation**

- **AEC Industry UAS Expert:** An ordinary privately controlled AEC flight is not a law-enforcement mission merely because a public client receives the deliverable. If law enforcement directs the collection or seeks access for an investigation, obtain written mission authority identifying the warrant or exception, purpose, area, duration, data handling, and responsible agency official before collection or disclosure.
- **Agency Practitioner:** Route the warrant or enumerated-exception determination through the agency's supervisory and legal process; a consultant cannot create that authority. The written mission order should identify the statutory basis, collection limits, notice or reporting duties, authorized users, retention and deletion, and the official accountable for the determination.
- **UAS Procurement Expert:** N/A — no procurement or equipment-selection implication identified
- **AEC Industry Legal Counsel:** Define in the contract whether the consultant is performing ordinary project work or acting at law-enforcement direction, and restrict secondary access or use accordingly. For covered work, retain the warrant or exception determination, mission order, acquisition metadata, disclosures, audit trail, retention, and destruction evidence.

### Minn. Stat. § 243.552 (2025), enacted 2020 c 110 s 5 — Unmanned Aerial Vehicle Prohibition (Correctional Facilities)
*Binding law or regulation | Current / in force*

**Objective Summary:** Minn. Stat. § 243.552, Subd. 2(a) makes it a misdemeanor to knowingly fly a UAV in the airspace over a state correctional facility, or over grounds belonging to or land controlled by the facility, without the written consent of the commissioner of corrections or a designee. Subd. 2(b) elevates the offense to a gross misdemeanor if the person additionally uses the UAV to record images (video or photographs) of the facility or its grounds, or to introduce or attempt to introduce non-contraband items as defined by the separate contraband statute, Minn. Stat. § 243.55. Subd. 1 defines UAV consistent with the standard 'no possibility of direct human intervention from within or on the aircraft' definition.

**Practical Interpretation**

- **AEC Industry UAS Expert:** Treat written facility coordination as a pre-mobilization gate for work within Statewide (state correctional facilities and their grounds); do not rely only on flight-app geofencing or a client's general site-access instruction. Include lost-link, return-to-home, emergency landing, sensor-direction, payload, and observer controls that prevent an unintended facility overflight, prohibited capture, delivery, or interference.
- **Agency Practitioner:** Route the request to the commissioner of corrections or authorized designee and issue written consent identifying the facility and controlled-land boundary, operator, aircraft, pilots, dates, route, purpose, payload, conditions, and field contact. Because the statute specifies no standard form or review time, confirm the current submission channel and lead time directly.
- **UAS Procurement Expert:** N/A — no procurement or equipment-selection implication identified
- **AEC Industry Legal Counsel:** Retain the commissioner's or designee's written consent, proof of delegated authority, boundary map, route, dates, aircraft, operating conditions, and amendments. Do not rely on a client's general property-access direction or flight-app geofence when the statute requires consent from the named corrections authority.

### Minn. Stat. § 360.55, Subd. 9-10 (2025); Subd. 9 added by 1Sp2021 c 5 art 4 s 111; Subd. 10 added by 1Sp2025 c 8 art 2 s 90 — Small Unmanned Aircraft System Registration Requirement
*Binding law or regulation | Current / in force*

**Objective Summary:** Minn. Stat. § 360.55, Subd. 9(a) requires a small unmanned aircraft system (unmanned aircraft under 55 lbs at takeoff, including payload) to be registered with the state for an annual $25 fee, unless the system is owned and operated solely for recreational purposes, in which case it is exempt from registration and fee. Subd. 9(b) exempts a qualifying system from the separate aircraft registration tax otherwise imposed under §§ 360.511-360.67. Subd. 10 separately requires an operator planning a 'coordinated unmanned aircraft system fleet' event to register the fleet at least 15 days beforehand, providing organizer contact information, event date/time/location, number of aircraft, proof of liability insurance, a copy of the operator's small UAS pilot license, and a copy of the commercial operator's license, subject to a $2-per-aircraft daily fee in lieu of the Subd. 9 fee.

**Practical Interpretation**

- **AEC Industry UAS Expert:** Make Minnesota registration status an aircraft-assignment gate for each small UAS operated in the state, accounting for takeoff weight with the planned payload. For a coordinated multi-UAS event, calendar the separate event registration at least 15 days in advance and assemble the required insurance, pilot-license, and commercial-license evidence.
- **Agency Practitioner:** MnDOT Office of Aeronautics should process the aircraft registration certificate through its current registration channel and verify the aircraft identity and applicable documentation. For coordinated fleet events, confirm timely filing at least 15 days before the event and retain the liability-insurance, pilot-license, and commercial-operator-license copies specified by statute.
- **UAS Procurement Expert:** Maintain an asset register linking each aircraft's serial number, takeoff weight with approved payloads, Minnesota registration certificate, renewal status, and event eligibility. Before purchasing or transferring a fleet asset into Minnesota service, confirm that its configuration can be registered and that documentation can be produced for coordinated fleet events.
- **AEC Industry Legal Counsel:** Retain the registration application, certificate, aircraft identity and weight record, renewals, and any MnDOT correspondence. For a coordinated fleet event, preserve the timely submission and attached insurance, pilot, and commercial-license documents rather than assuming ordinary aircraft registration covers the event filing.

### MnDOT official guidance (dot.state.mn.us/aero/drones/commercial.html), citing Minn. Stat. § 360.075 as the licensing authority — Commercial UAS Operators — Commercial Operations License
*Official agency policy | Current (as posted)*

**Objective Summary:** MnDOT's official commercial-operator guidance states that Minnesota statutes require a license when a person advertises or holds themselves out as providing a service involving aircraft, including drones, and that the determination of whether a Commercial Operations License (in addition to aircraft registration) is required turns on a case-by-case test of whether flight outputs are provided to a third party. MnDOT's illustrative examples: a drone flight whose photos are delivered to a client requires both registration and licensing, even with no separate charge for drone use; a drone flight used only for internal sightline planning (with client deliverables produced by other means) requires registration but not licensing. The page directs applicants to MnDOT's separate Commercial Operations licensing page and lists special drone-specific instructions for the application (e.g., entering 'Private-UAV' for the operating airport field).

**Practical Interpretation**

- **AEC Industry UAS Expert:** Obtain and keep the MnDOT commercial operations license current before advertising or performing Minnesota drone services that deliver flight-derived output to a client. Treat licensing as an operator-level business prerequisite rather than a mission-specific authorization, and separately complete aircraft registration, insurance, airspace, and site permissions.
- **Agency Practitioner:** Use MnDOT Office of Aeronautics' current licensing portal to evaluate the commercial-operator application and supporting business, insurance, and aircraft information required by current guidance. Identify renewal and correction requirements clearly; the license does not itself approve a particular site or flight.
- **UAS Procurement Expert:** N/A — no procurement or equipment-selection implication identified
- **AEC Industry Legal Counsel:** Retain the application, issued license, renewals, insurance, business identity, and MnDOT correspondence, and ensure proposals do not advertise covered service before licensure. Contract language should distinguish this commercial-operator license from aircraft registration and mission-specific property or airspace authority.

### Minn. Stat. § 97B.085, Subd. 1 (Using Radios to Take Animals); Minn. Stat. § 97B.081 (Using Artificial Lights to Locate Animals); Minn. Stat. § 97B.086 (Possessing Night Vision or Thermal Imaging Equipment); as applied to drones per DNR enforcement guidance — Hunting-Related Drone Restrictions (Radio Equipment, Artificial Light, and Thermal/Night-Vision Equipment Applied to UAS)
*Binding law or regulation, as applied to UAS by official agency guidance | Current / in force*

**Objective Summary:** Minn. Stat. § 97B.085, Subd. 1 prohibits using 'radio equipment' to take big or small game. Minn. Stat. § 97B.081 prohibits casting artificial light (spotlight, headlight, or similar) to spot, locate, or take a wild animal, subject to enumerated exceptions (e.g., licensed bear/deer-hunter tracking, certain fox/coyote hunting). Minn. Stat. § 97B.086 prohibits possessing night-vision or thermal-imaging equipment while taking wild animals or while possessing an implement that could be used to do so, subject to exceptions for peace officers/military and certain coyote/fox hunters outside firearms deer season. None of these three statutes mentions 'drone' or 'unmanned aircraft' in its own text. The DNR's own official enforcement guidance document, 'Drone use while hunting' (files.dnr.state.mn.us), states directly that 'it is not legal to use drones to take big or small game, which includes using drones to recover dead or wounded game,' and cites § 97B.085, Subd. 1 (radio equipment) together with §§ 97B.081 and 97B.086 (artificial light; thermal/night-vision) as the applicable authorities for that position.

**Practical Interpretation**

- **AEC Industry UAS Expert:** Screen missions near active hunting so thermal, night-vision, artificial-light, or live-location capability is not used to locate or take wildlife or assist another person doing so. Document the infrastructure, survey, or environmental purpose and keep collected wildlife information out of hunting decisions.
- **Agency Practitioner:** N/A — no agency process involved
- **UAS Procurement Expert:** N/A — no procurement or equipment-selection implication identified
- **AEC Industry Legal Counsel:** Preserve the legitimate project purpose, active-hunting screening, payload configuration, lighting, route, imagery recipients, and any stop-work decision. Escalate a request to use thermal, night-vision, artificial light, radio control, or live drone data to locate or take wildlife; ownership of the sensor does not determine whether the prohibited use occurred.

### Minn. R. 6100.2200 (Aircraft in State Parks); DNR official state park rules page — Unmanned Aircraft Systems/Drones — State Park and Recreation Area Rules
*Binding law or regulation (rule) plus official agency policy (permit practice) | Current / in force*

**Objective Summary:** Minn. R. 6100.2200 makes it unlawful for any person to land an aircraft on land or water totally within the boundaries of a state park, except by permit, at a designated airport/landing strip, in an emergency, during rescue operations, or while conducting official federal/state/county business. The DNR's own official state park rules page (dnr.state.mn.us/state_parks/rules.html) expressly states that unmanned aircraft/drones are 'aircraft' under the FAA's definition and that, because landing is prohibited, operating a UAS in these areas is 'not practical'; the Division 'discourages' UAS use in state parks, recreation areas, and waysides due to landing impracticality and stated concerns about wildlife, DNR infrastructure, and visitor safety/privacy/experience. The same DNR page separately requires a film/photography permit whenever a project 'involves the use of an aircraft, including unmanned aircraft (drone),' regardless of whether other permit triggers apply, with the aircraft/drone-specific application routed through the DNR public relations supervisor and a review period of up to 30 days.

**Practical Interpretation**

- **AEC Industry UAS Expert:** Treat property authorization for State parks, state recreation areas, and state waysides managed by the Minnesota DNR Parks and Trails Division as a pre-mobilization gate and obtain conditions that cover launch, landing, route, dates, aircraft, crew, and sensor purpose. Confirm whether the approval is site-wide or location-specific and carry the written authorization and field contact with the crew.
- **Agency Practitioner:** Route filming, photography, or videography requests involving an aircraft or drone through the park or area supervisor and, when applicable, the DNR public-relations supervisor. Allow up to 30 days for review and issue written conditions identifying the park unit, launch and landing areas, route, dates, aircraft, purpose, insurance, resource protections, and field contact.
- **UAS Procurement Expert:** N/A — no procurement or equipment-selection implication identified
- **AEC Industry Legal Counsel:** Retain the application, written DNR approval, property map, conditions, insurance, amendments, and communications with the reviewing supervisor. Confirm whether the mission is governed by the aircraft-landing rule, DNR filming policy, or both, and do not infer approval from ordinary park access.

### University of Minnesota Administrative Policy, 'Use of Unmanned Aircraft Systems (Drones),' effective November 2016 — Use of Unmanned Aircraft Systems (Drones) — University of Minnesota Administrative Policy
*Official agency policy | Current / in force*

**Objective Summary:** The University of Minnesota's administrative policy on UAS use, effective November 2016, requires anyone operating a UAS on University property or in University airspace to obtain approval from the University's UAS Safety Board (UASSB) via a program plan submitted at least 5 business days in advance, except for use inside an enclosed classroom or research lab. A request for public-relations, communications, or commercial UAS use on the University's behalf requires review and approval by University Marketing Communications, University Services, and the relevant campus law enforcement agency, and commercial operators must have completed all required federal, state, and local registration/licensure requirements. The policy prohibits UAS use for recreation or any other non-mission-related purpose on University property, and imposes a stadium-specific blackout (3 hours before/during/after main-bowl events at Huntington Bank Stadium and Malosky Stadium, extending to all East Bank/West Bank/Saint Paul/Duluth campus property and airspace). All UAS on University property, regardless of ownership, must be registered with the FAA and the MnDOT Office of Aeronautics. The University holds a blanket FAA Certificate of Waiver or Authorization (COA); where University policy and FAA regulation diverge, the more restrictive standard governs.

**Practical Interpretation**

- **AEC Industry UAS Expert:** Treat property authorization for University of Minnesota system property and airspace above it (Twin Cities, Duluth, Morris, Crookston, Rochester campuses) as a pre-mobilization gate and obtain conditions that cover launch, landing, route, dates, aircraft, crew, and sensor purpose. Confirm whether the approval is site-wide or location-specific and carry the written authorization and field contact with the crew.
- **Agency Practitioner:** Route the program plan to the University UAS Safety Board at least five business days before the activity and coordinate the additional marketing, University Services, and campus law-enforcement reviews required for commercial or public-relations use. The written decision should identify campus, operating area, aircraft, pilots, dates, purpose, insurance, conditions, and field contact.
- **UAS Procurement Expert:** N/A — no procurement or equipment-selection implication identified
- **AEC Industry Legal Counsel:** Retain the program plan, UAS Safety Board decision, all additional commercial-use reviews, property and airspace documentation, insurance, conditions, and amendments. Confirm whether the approval covers every campus, date, operator, and data use in the scope rather than treating a University project contract as flight authorization.
