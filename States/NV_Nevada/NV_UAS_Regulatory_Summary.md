## 1. State UAS Regulatory Overview

Nevada has a detailed state UAS framework layered on federal aviation law. It regulates low-altitude interference and landings, creates a repeat-overflight trespass action with a conditional exception for Nevada-registered businesses and land surveyors, imposes fixed buffers around critical facilities, and retains a five-mile airport consent-or-FAA-authorization rule. Separate statutes govern public- and law-enforcement-agency operations, registration, surveillance, evidence, and equipment procurement. State Parks, pesticide-applicator rules, wildlife rules, and the University of Nevada, Reno add mission-specific or property-specific controls. No separate general Nevada commercial-UAS operating permit was located.

## 2. Statewide UAS Laws and Regulations

### NV-001 — Lawfulness of flight and landing; liability for surface injury

**Authority:** Nev. Rev. Stat. §§ 493.020, 493.050-.060  
**Status:** Current / in force

Nevada treats a UAV as an aircraft for the Uniform State Law for Aeronautics. Flight over land or water is lawful unless it is so low that it interferes with the owner’s existing use, is imminently dangerous to persons or property below, or violates another provision of NRS 493.010-.120 or an implementing regulation. A voluntary landing on another’s land or water requires consent. For injuries caused by ascent, descent, flight, or a dropped object, the owner—and a lessee when applicable—is presumptively liable, subject to rebuttal; a nonowner operator is liable only for the operator’s own negligence.

### NV-002 — Dangerous, intoxicated, careless, or reckless UAV operation; federal credentials

**Authority:** Nev. Rev. Stat. §§ 493.100, 493.130-.160, 493.200  
**Status:** Current / in force

NRS 493.100 makes dangerous conduct over a heavily populated area or public gathering a misdemeanor, including low flight that endangers people and dropping an object with reckless disregard and willful indifference. NRS 493.130 separately makes intoxicated or careless or reckless aircraft operation that endangers life or property a gross misdemeanor and expressly includes UAVs; courts consider federal safe-operation standards when applying that rule. Nevada also requires any federally required aircraft certificate and operator certificate, permit, rating, or license. The park-specific exception to the acrobatic-flight clause does not excuse reckless operation or override a park’s separate drone restriction.

**Practical Interpretation**

- **AEC Industry UAS Expert:** Screen aircraft and payload configuration against Dangerous, intoxicated, careless, or reckless UAV operation; federal credentials before deployment, including release devices, tethered tools, sample systems, and experimental attachments. Use configuration control so a field crew cannot inadvertently deploy a prohibited or ambiguous payload.

### NV-003 — UAV trespass action and registered-business or land-surveyor exception

**Authority:** Nev. Rev. Stat. § 493.103  
**Status:** Current / in force

A Nevada property owner or lawful occupant may sue a UAV owner or operator for trespass when the aircraft flies below 250 feet over the property after at least one prior below-250-foot flight and after the operator has been notified that such flight is unauthorized. The action is unavailable for airport flight-path, takeoff, landing, compliant government, and certain commercial operations. A business registered in Nevada or a land surveyor qualifies for the commercial exception only when the operator has FAA authority, the flight is within lawful business or surveying activity, and it does not unreasonably interfere with the property’s existing use. Available relief includes treble injury damages and potentially fees, costs, and an injunction.

### NV-004 — UAV operation near critical facilities and within five miles of airports

**Authority:** Nev. Rev. Stat. §§ 493.020(2), 493.109  
**Status:** Current / in force

Nevada prohibits UAV operation within 500 horizontal feet or 250 vertical feet of a defined critical facility without the facility owner’s written consent. Covered sites include many energy, chemical, pipeline, water and wastewater, mining, electric-transmission, detention, prison, and corrections facilities, excluding underground utility infrastructure. Operation within five miles of a qualifying public-use airport requires consent from the airport authority or operator unless the operator has an applicable FAA waiver, exemption, or other authorization. The operator must carry the supporting documentation throughout the operation. A violation is a misdemeanor, so both site classification and the operative airport authorization should be documented before flight.

### NV-005 — Weaponization of unmanned aerial vehicles prohibited

**Authority:** Nev. Rev. Stat. § 493.106  
**Status:** Current / in force

Nevada prohibits both weaponizing an unmanned aerial vehicle and operating a weaponized unmanned aerial vehicle. Either act is a category D felony. A person who weaponizes a UAV in violation of the statute and discharges the weapon commits a category C felony. The section states no permitting exception. Commercial teams should therefore scrutinize unusual payloads, release mechanisms, deterrent devices, and client-requested attachments before deployment rather than assuming that a nonfirearm device falls outside the prohibition; the statute itself does not define the word “weaponize,” so characterization can be fact sensitive.

### NV-006 — Law-enforcement UAV warrants, exceptions, evidence limits, and equipment restrictions

**Authority:** Nev. Rev. Stat. § 493.112  
**Status:** Current / in force

A Nevada law-enforcement agency generally needs a warrant, limited to no more than 10 days unless renewed, to gather evidence or information by UAV within a residence’s curtilage or another place where a person has a reasonable expectation of privacy. Exceptions cover specified exigencies, detailed written consent, search and rescue, documented imminent threats, and declared emergencies or disasters subject to geographic and purpose limits. Information obtained contrary to the section is inadmissible and cannot support reasonable suspicion or probable cause. Law-enforcement agencies also must comply with the Department of Public Safety’s current equipment and service restrictions, subject to the statutory treatment of pre-effective-date acquisitions.

**Practical Interpretation**

### NV-007 — Public-agency UAV registration, authorized purposes, and use limitations

**Authority:** Nev. Rev. Stat. §§ 493.115-.118; Nev. Admin. Code § 493.100; LCB File R111-23  
**Status:** Current / in force

A Nevada public agency may operate a UAV only after registering it with the Department of Public Safety and in accordance with DPS regulations. Authorized purposes include surveying and mapping, inspection of public land and infrastructure, natural-resource and environmental evaluation, hazardous-material inspection, emergency functions, research, training, and other listed public uses. A public agency may not use its UAV to assist law enforcement or conduct a criminal prosecution. Scheduled building- or fire-code inspection is authorized, but the agency may not collect photographs, images, or recordings during that inspection, and improperly collected material is restricted. The registration submission identifies the agency, aircraft, operators, and intended use.

### NV-008 — Restricted UAS vendors, equipment, and services for public agencies

**Authority:** Nev. Rev. Stat. §§ 493.112(5), 493.115(3), 493.118(6)-(10); LCB File R111-23  
**Status:** Current / in force

Nevada public and law-enforcement agencies may not operate, purchase, or acquire UAS, related equipment, or services contrary to DPS restrictions. The statutory lists incorporate specified Department of Defense entities and their products, Nevada technology-office prohibitions, and other DPS determinations; pre-January 1, 2025 acquisitions have a statutory operating grandfather. Effective July 1, 2025, an agency may acquire from a business or entity otherwise on the vendor list if related information is stored and maintained exclusively in the United States, inaccessible to third parties, the agency determines the vendor’s policies comply, and federal requirements—including funding restrictions—are met. U.S.-manufactured systems are encouraged where procurement law allows.

## 3. State Agency UAS Requirements

### NV-009 — State Parks drone prohibition and special-use permit exception

**Authority:** Nevada State Parks FAQ; related Nev. Admin. Code §§ 407.050, 407.125  
**Status:** Current agency policy

Nevada State Parks states that drone use is prohibited unless the park supervisor has designated an area for that use or the operator receives a special-use permit for unmanned-aircraft use; FAA rules continue to apply. The agency position aligns with NAC 407.125, which bars aircraft takeoff or landing from park land or water not specifically approved by the park supervisor, and with the Division’s permit authority. A commercial imagery project may also trigger the separate photography-permit framework based on its commercial nature, equipment, personnel, resource impacts, access needs, or closure requirements. Project teams should obtain park-specific written direction because designation and permit conditions can vary.

### NV-010 — UAV aerial-pesticide applicator licensing and pilot qualifications

**Authority:** Nev. Admin. Code §§ 555.280, 555.283, 555.380  
**Status:** Current / in force

Nevada classifies use of an aircraft, expressly including a UAV, to apply pesticides on water, rangeland, crops, forest, idle cropland, and associated noncropland as the aerial field of commercial pest control. A commercial applicator must apply for the appropriate field and category and pass the required examinations. An operator, including an aerial operator, must prove examination completion and employment by a Nevada-licensed pest-control business. When the application aircraft is a UAV, the aerial operator must hold a remote-pilot certificate with the appropriate UAS rating under Part 107. These rules are additional to pesticide-label, business-license, and applicable federal agricultural-aircraft requirements.

### NV-011 — Use of UAVs to harass, locate, or observe wildlife for hunting or trapping

**Authority:** Nev. Rev. Stat. § 503.010; Nev. Admin. Code § 503.148  
**Status:** Current / in force

Nevada prohibits harassing game mammals or game birds by manned or unmanned aircraft, shooting from such aircraft, and spotting or locating game by aircraft and communicating the information within 24 hours for hunting or trapping. NAC 503.148 separately prohibits using a UAV, during July 1 through the last day of February, to locate or observe specified game or fur-bearing wildlife for hunting or trapping in designated management units, subject to stated regulatory exceptions. The restriction is purpose based: unrelated environmental, engineering, or mapping work is not automatically hunting assistance, but collected wildlife-location information should not be repurposed or shared to facilitate a prohibited hunt.

### NV-012 — Policy on Use of Uncrewed Aircraft Systems (UAS) and Drones

**Authority:** University Administrative Manual Policy 5,030  
**Status:** Current university policy

The University of Nevada, Reno prohibits any party from flying UAS in or above University property without prior written approval from the Vice President for Research and Innovation. A complete Operations Plan is due at least seven calendar days before flight and must address the pilot, aircraft, site, airspace, purpose, authorizations, data security, safety, roads, emergency procedures, and other specified subjects. A non-University operator must provide liability coverage satisfactory to the University naming it as an additional insured or an acceptable written indemnity. The policy also requires preflight and postflight notification and separately controls University-related operations and University-funded UAS purchases.

## 4. Non-Regulatory Context

Nevada hosts an FAA-designated UAS Test Site managed through Nevada Autonomous at the University of Nevada, Reno. That program can support testing, airworthiness, operational planning, certificates of authorization, and advanced research, but its existence does not itself authorize an ordinary commercial mission or displace the state statutes, agency-property rules, or federal approvals applicable to a particular flight.

## 5. Unresolved Operational Questions

1. **Trespass exception:** Is the operator’s business registered in Nevada, is the mission within its lawful activities, and could repeated low-altitude flight unreasonably interfere with the property’s existing use?
2. **Critical-facility classification:** Does the site or an adjacent asset meet Nevada’s detailed definition, and has the actual owner supplied written consent covering the planned geometry?
3. **Airport documentation:** Which public-use airports are within five miles, what consent or FAA authorization supports the mission, and what documentation will the pilot carry?
4. **Public-agency status:** Will a client treat contractor-owned equipment and contractor pilots as a UAV the agency operates, requiring DPS registration and compliance with the authorized-purpose rules?
5. **Procurement stack:** Does the aircraft, component, software, cloud service, manufacturer, distributor, or funding source trigger a DPS, Nevada technology-office, Department of Defense, or federal restriction, and does the 2025 data-residency exception actually apply?
6. **State property:** Has the controlling park, university, transportation, or other state-property manager issued current written approval, and are separate filming, occupancy, insurance, or indemnity terms required?
7. **Specialized payloads:** Does a treatment, release, or other non-imaging payload trigger pesticide licensing, weaponization concerns, or additional state permits?
