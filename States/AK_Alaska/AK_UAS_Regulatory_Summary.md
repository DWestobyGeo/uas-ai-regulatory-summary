## 1. State UAS Regulatory Overview

Alaska's UAS-specific statutory framework is narrower than most states researched so far: its only dedicated UAS statute (AS 18.65.900–.902) governs law-enforcement use, not private or commercial operators. Two administrative-code provisions matter most for AEC work: a Board of Game rule (5 AAC 92.080(7)(G)–(I)) that bars using a UAS to spot or locate game statewide, and a Division of Parks and Outdoor Recreation drone-use stipulation applied to commercial permits on state park land, layered on top of a separate, textually ambiguous Chugach State Park aircraft rule. A 2024 Alaska Supreme Court decision, *State v. McKelvey*, sets a constitutional privacy framework for aerial surveillance with optics that is widely expected to extend to drones, though the case itself involved a manned aircraft. Alaska has not enacted a statewide UAS-preemption statute, so municipal ordinances (e.g., Anchorage's park rules) may matter more here than in preemption states.

## 2. Statewide UAS Laws and Regulations

### AS 18.65.900–18.65.902 — Law Enforcement Use of Unmanned Aircraft Systems
*Binding law | Current, enacted 2014*

**Objective Summary:** AS 18.65.900 prohibits a law enforcement agency from using a UAS except as provided in AS 18.65.900–.909. AS 18.65.901 requires each agency to adopt written procedures: FAA authorization; trained/certified pilots and supervising officials; approval by the commissioner/deputy commissioner of public safety or the agency's chief administrative officer; a public purpose; an auditable flight-log record; a public-notification method; and community involvement in policy development. AS 18.65.902 permits UAS use only (1) to gather evidence in a criminal investigation under a warrant or recognized exception, or (2) for non-criminal uses that do not unreasonably invade privacy and comply with the AS 18.65.901 procedures.

### 5 AAC 92.080(7)(G)–(I) — Unlawful Methods of Taking Game: Airborne, Remote, or Wireless Spotting Devices
*Binding regulation | Current, effective July 1, 2025*

**Objective Summary:** Prohibits taking game with the aid of a device that has been airborne, is controlled remotely, or communicates wirelessly and is used to spot or locate game with a camera or video device, and separately bars any camera or sensory device capable of sending messages wirelessly. Wireless communication may not be used to take a specific animal until 3:00 a.m. the day after the device was used, with narrow ground-based-communication exceptions for specified bison and moose hunts. Carried into the big-game-specific methods list at 5 AAC 92.085. Originated from a 2014 Board of Game action after drones were found being used to spot and assist in taking big game.

## 3. State Agency and Facility-Specific UAS Requirements

### Division of Parks and Outdoor Recreation — Drone Use Stipulations for Commercial Filming Permits
*Permit condition | Current, Rev. 1.15.2026*

**Objective Summary:** The Division's standard stipulations for Commercial Filming Permits include a dedicated Drone Use provision: no operation in high-density public-use areas (campgrounds, picnic areas, trailheads, developed facilities); FAA Part 107 compliance and registration where required; a 400 ft altitude cap and 100 ft standoff from obstacles, people, or wildlife; visual-line-of-sight operation; no flight near other aircraft, airports, or emergency-response efforts; no use to follow, chase, harass, or alter wildlife behavior; no use by anyone to spot game or assist another person's taking of game; and a requirement to produce recorded imagery/data to law enforcement on request. Enforceable as a citation under 11 AAC 18.030(c); may result in permit suspension or revocation.

**Practical Interpretation**

- **AEC Industry UAS Expert:** Plan environmental and mapping flights to avoid conduct that could be characterized as locating, pursuing, disturbing, or harassing wildlife. Coordinate mission timing and stand-off distances with the land manager when project work overlaps sensitive habitat or hunting activity.

### State v. McKelvey — Constitutional Aerial-Surveillance Framework
*Court decision | Alaska Supreme Court, No. S-17910, decided March 8, 2024*

**Objective Summary:** The Alaska Supreme Court held that warrantless aerial surveillance of a private backyard, using a zoom lens from an airplane, violated the Alaska Constitution's search-and-seizure protections. The case involved a fixed-wing aircraft and telephoto lens, not a UAS; a concurrence argued for an even broader warrant requirement covering any aerial observation, and commentary on the decision frames it as relevant precedent for future drone-surveillance disputes.

## 5. Unresolved Operational Questions

- **11 AAC 20.020 UAS application:** Whether "aircraft" in the Chugach State Park rule is understood by the Division to include a UAS/drone was not confirmed by agency guidance in this pass (compare AK-005, where the Division's own commercial-filming stipulations do expressly regulate drones statewide on park land).
- **AK-005 scope beyond filming permits:** Whether a non-filming AEC commercial UAS survey/mapping/inspection flight on Division-managed park land requires an equivalent permit and is bound by the same drone stipulations under the Division's general commercial-use permit authority (11 AAC 18.010) was not separately confirmed.
- **McKelvey's application to UAS:** The case is a manned-aircraft precedent; its extension to drone-specific fact patterns has not been tested in an Alaska court.
- **Local ordinances:** Out of scope for this phase; may be more operationally significant in Alaska than in preemption states given the absence of a statewide preemption statute.
