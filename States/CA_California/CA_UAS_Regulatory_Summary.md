# California — Commercial UAS Regulatory Summary

**Prepared for:** AEC (surveying, mapping, construction, inspection) UAS program management
**Research date:** August 1, 2026 | **Version:** 1.0
**Scope note:** Federal FAA Part 107 is the baseline for all commercial sUAS operations nationwide and is not restated here. Per current research-phase scope (Agent Instructions v4), this summary covers California **state and state-agency** UAS authorities only — local (municipal/county) ordinances and tribal rules are deferred to a later phase and are not included here. **Unlike Texas, California has not enacted a statute preempting local UAS ordinances** (see Section 4), so the local-ordinance layer deferred here is likely to matter more in California than in preemption states — factor that into any project-specific reliance on this document. Full citations and metadata are in `CA_UAS_Source_Register.csv`.

Each rule below is presented as an **Objective Summary** (binding text/citation, no interpretation) followed by **Practical Interpretation** from two labeled perspectives: an AEC industry UAS expert (operational read) and AEC industry legal counsel (risk/compliance read). Practical interpretation is not legal advice.

## 1. State UAS Regulatory Overview

California regulates UAS through a set of targeted statutes rather than one comprehensive UAS code: a civil privacy tort extended to drones (Civil Code § 1708.8), a criminal emergency-scene-interference statute expressly covering remote drone operators (Penal Code § 402), a corrections-facility restriction (Penal Code § 4577), wildlife-harassment and hunting-related prohibitions (14 CCR §§ 251, 251.1, 550), and a civil-immunity provision protecting public entities from liability for damaging an interfering drone (Government Code §§ 853–853.5). State agencies with significant AEC-relevant UAS programs include Caltrans (which requires a separate encroachment permit for any UAS activity within State Highway System right-of-way) and California State Parks (which requires permits for commercial, research, and public-agency UAS use and bans motorized equipment, including UAS, in designated wilderness/natural/cultural preserves). California has no enacted statute restricting UAS by manufacturer/country of origin, and — in contrast to Texas — has no state-law preemption of local UAS ordinances, meaning the municipal/county layer (out of scope this phase) carries more independent weight here.

## 2. Statewide UAS Laws and Regulations

### Cal. Civil Code § 1708.8 — Physical and Constructive Invasion of Privacy
*Binding law | Current, amended 2016 to reach device-based capture including drones*

**Objective Summary:** Creates civil liability for (a) "physical invasion of privacy" — knowingly entering the land or airspace above another's land without permission, or otherwise trespassing, to capture an image/recording of a person engaging in private, personal, or familial activity in an offensive manner — and (b) "constructive invasion of privacy" — using any device, including a drone, to attempt such a capture in an offensive manner where the capture could not have been achieved without a trespass but for use of the device, regardless of physical trespass. Liability is up to 3x general/special damages plus possible punitive damages and disgorgement of commercial proceeds; a separate $5,000–$50,000 civil fine applies for a commercial-purpose violation. Subdivision (g) exempts law-enforcement/governmental personnel conducting an authorized investigation supported by articulable suspicion.

**Practical Interpretation**
- *AEC Industry UAS Expert Perspective:* Even without physically trespassing, capturing offensive/private imagery via drone that could only have been obtained through trespass but for the device can trigger "constructive invasion of privacy" liability — plan flight paths and camera framing to avoid capturing identifiable people engaged in private activity on adjoining residential parcels.
- *AEC Industry Legal Counsel Perspective:* This is a civil tort with treble damages, punitive damages, and a $5,000–$50,000 statutory fine exposure — document the professional purpose of any flight capturing imagery near residential property, and consider blurring/redaction workflows for incidentally captured individuals.

### Cal. Penal Code § 402 — Remaining at Scene of Emergency to Impede Personnel (drone-inclusive)
*Binding law | Current, amended 2016 to expressly cover remote drone operators*

**Objective Summary:** It is a misdemeanor to go to or stop at the scene of an emergency to view emergency personnel's activities and thereby impede them, unless viewing is part of the person's job duties. Subdivision (a)(2), added in 2016, expressly extends the section to "a person, regardless of his or her location, who operates or uses an unmanned aerial vehicle, remote piloted aircraft, or drone that is at the scene of an emergency" — the remote pilot need not be physically present to be liable. "Emergency" is broadly defined (fires, explosions, crashes, flooding, windstorm damage, accidents, spills, etc.).

**Practical Interpretation**
- *AEC Industry UAS Expert Perspective:* Treat active fire, crash, or disaster scenes as an automatic no-fly for any UAS mission until coordinating directly with incident command — CAL FIRE and other agencies actively enforce this, and a remote pilot can be liable even when not physically on scene.
- *AEC Industry Legal Counsel Perspective:* Document incident-command coordination and any professional/job-duty basis for flying near an active emergency before tasking a crew; the job-duties exception is narrow and doesn't automatically cover general damage-assessment work absent an agency request.

### Cal. Gov't Code §§ 853–853.5 — Unmanned Aircraft: Public Entity/Employee Immunity for Damage to Interfering Drones
*Binding law | Current*

**Objective Summary:** A local public entity or employee is not liable for damage to a UAS caused while providing (and the UAS was interfering with) emergency medical/ambulance services, firefighting services, or search-and-rescue services (including associated air services). This immunity supplements other existing immunities for emergency personnel/vehicles.

**Practical Interpretation**
- *AEC Industry UAS Expert Perspective:* There is no state-law recourse against a public agency if your firm's UAS is damaged or disabled while interfering with an active emergency operation — an additional, independent reason (beyond Penal Code § 402) to maintain a strict no-fly posture near active emergency operations.
- *AEC Industry Legal Counsel Perspective:* Advise clients/insurers that equipment losses from interference with covered emergency services are unlikely to be recoverable from the responding public entity; document any pre-coordinated, authorized flight near an emergency operation to distinguish it from an interfering one.

### Cal. Penal Code § 4577 — Unauthorized Operation of Unmanned Aircraft System Over Prison, Jail, or Juvenile Facility
*Binding law | Current, enacted 2018*

**Objective Summary:** A person who knowingly and intentionally operates a UAS on or above the grounds of a state prison, county jail, or juvenile hall/camp/ranch is guilty of an infraction punishable by a $500 fine. Exempt: a person employed by the facility operating within the scope of employment, or a person with prior permission from CDCR (state prisons), the county sheriff (jails), or the operating county department (juvenile facilities).

**Practical Interpretation**
- *AEC Industry UAS Expert Perspective:* Identify any state prison, county jail, or juvenile detention facility near a project site during pre-flight planning; obtain prior written permission from the appropriate agency before flying on or above facility grounds.
- *AEC Industry Legal Counsel Perspective:* This is a low-level infraction, but document the prior-permission exemption relied upon for any flight over or above a covered facility's grounds given the facility-security sensitivity involved.

### 14 CCR §§ 251, 251.1 — Prohibition on Using Aircraft (Including Drones) to Pursue or Harass Wildlife
*Binding administrative regulation | Current*

**Objective Summary:** 14 CCR § 251 prohibits pursuing, driving, herding, or taking any bird or mammal from a motor-driven air vehicle, and separately bars using any flight-capable device to locate big-game mammals within 48 hours of a big-game season. 14 CCR § 251.1 prohibits harassing (an intentional act disrupting normal behavior patterns, including breeding, feeding, or sheltering) any game or nongame bird or mammal by drone or otherwise.

**Practical Interpretation**
- *AEC Industry UAS Expert Perspective:* Plan environmental-monitoring, habitat-mapping, or resource-inventory flights to avoid disrupting normal wildlife behavior and avoid anything that could be read as locating big-game animals near a hunting-season window.
- *AEC Industry Legal Counsel Perspective:* Document the survey/mapping purpose of any flight over wildlife habitat to distinguish it from a "harassment" use; the standard is judgment-based, so build a conservative standoff buffer near known wildlife concentrations into standard operating procedure.

### AB 1749 (2025–2026, pending) — Interfering with Wildfire Suppression and Emergency Responses with Drone
*Proposed or pending authority | Not yet enacted as of August 1, 2026*

**Objective Summary:** Would add a new Civil Code section creating a civil penalty of up to $75,000 per violation for operating a UAS at an emergency scene in a way that impedes response personnel, enforceable by the Attorney General, a county counsel, or a city attorney. As of the most recent tracked action (mid-June 2026), the bill remained pending in Senate policy committees. As tracked, it includes an exemption for pilots holding an FAA Part 107 operational waiver.

**Practical Interpretation**
- *AEC Industry UAS Expert Perspective:* This is not yet law — don't treat the $75,000 figure as current exposure, but track the bill given the existing criminal exposure under Penal Code § 402 for the same conduct.
- *AEC Industry Legal Counsel Perspective:* Monitor AB 1749 through the remainder of the session; if enacted, the civil-penalty and AG/city-attorney enforcement structure would materially increase exposure for UAS operation near an active wildfire or emergency scene beyond the existing misdemeanor.

## 3. State Agency UAS Requirements

### Transportation — California Department of Transportation (Caltrans) Encroachment Permit
*Official agency policy (permit-backed) | Current*

**Objective Summary:** Operating a UAS from within the State Highway System (SHS) right-of-way requires a Caltrans encroachment permit — before launching, landing, or entering the SHS right-of-way to operate or participate in UAS-related activities, regardless of where personnel are physically located or where the aircraft is flown. Encroachment permits are considered only for authorized construction work or other permitted activities such as commercial filming, special events, surveys, or accident reconstruction; recreational, private, or general business UAS use in the right-of-way is not allowed. Caltrans contractors/consultants must also coordinate current-project UAS use with the Division of Aeronautics and the project's Caltrans contract coordinator.

**Practical Interpretation**
- *AEC Industry UAS Expert Perspective:* For any highway/bridge corridor survey, mapping, or inspection project, submit the Standard Encroachment Permit Application (TR-0100) to the local District Encroachment Permits Office well before mobilization, and separately coordinate with the Division of Aeronautics on Caltrans-scoped task orders.
- *AEC Industry Legal Counsel Perspective:* Confirm the encroachment permit is in hand — not merely applied for — before authorizing any right-of-way launch, landing, or entry, and confirm the underlying activity fits a category Caltrans will actually permit.

### Parks and Recreation — California State Parks (UAS Guidance)
*Official agency policy (permit-backed) + binding regulation | Current*

**Objective Summary:** Drones are generally allowed in State Parks, Beaches, Historic Parks, Recreation Areas, and Vehicular Recreation Areas except where prohibited by a District Superintendent's posted order — always check with the local District. State Park regulations impose a blanket ban on motorized equipment, including UAS, within designated wilderness areas, natural preserves, and cultural preserves regardless of posted orders (14 CCR § 4351). Commercial drone use requires a permit, with FAA authorization submitted to the District and, depending on use, a Special Event permit, Right of Entry permit, or a California Film Commission permit (14 CCR § 4316) for filming. Research UAS use requires FAA approval plus a DPR 65 scientific collection permit; public-agency use requires submission of the operator's FAA Certificate of Waiver or Authorization (COA).

**Practical Interpretation**
- *AEC Industry UAS Expert Perspective:* Before any state-park AEC UAS work, check for unit-specific posted orders with the local District and route the FAA authorization plus the applicable permit (Special Event, Right of Entry, DPR 65, or Film Commission) through the District Superintendent well in advance.
- *AEC Industry Legal Counsel Perspective:* Retain the FAA authorization submission and the specific park-issued permit in the project file; confirm which permit track applies since required documentation differs by category (commercial vs. research vs. public-agency).

### Fish and Wildlife — CDFW-Administered Lands (Special Use Permit)
*Binding administrative regulation | Current*

**Objective Summary:** 14 CCR § 550 bars operating a UAS on CDFW-administered land (wildlife areas, ecological reserves) except under a Special Use Permit issued by the Department.

**Practical Interpretation**
- *AEC Industry UAS Expert Perspective:* Treat any CDFW wildlife area or ecological reserve the same as a state park for launch/land/operate purposes — apply for a Special Use Permit before staging environmental-monitoring or habitat-mapping work, and build permit lead time into scheduling.
- *AEC Industry Legal Counsel Perspective:* Retain the CDFW Special Use Permit in the project file; confirm current requirements directly with CDFW given the secondary-source citation noted in the confidence summary below.

### Procurement — No Enacted UAS Manufacturer/Country-of-Origin Restriction
*Research result — no current statute; failed 2023–2024 bill noted*

**Objective Summary:** No California statute currently restricts state or local agency UAS procurement by manufacturer or country of origin. SB 99 (2023–2024, Sen. Umberg) would have barred state/local agencies from purchasing UAS from manufacturers barred from U.S. Department of Defense use (understood to include DJI); it passed the Senate but was held in Assembly committee on August 15, 2024 and did not become law. A different, unrelated SB 99 exists in the 2025–2026 session under a different author.

**Practical Interpretation**
- *AEC Industry UAS Expert Perspective:* Do not build California-specific DJI/Chinese-manufacturer procurement restrictions into standard operating procedure — no such state statute is in force, unlike the executive-branch prohibited-technologies list some other states (e.g., Texas) apply to state-owned devices.
- *AEC Industry Legal Counsel Perspective:* Do not represent to a client that California law restricts UAS manufacturer choice for state/local agency work; a specific public-agency contract could independently restrict equipment by manufacturer as a contract term, but that is not a general statutory requirement.

### Professional Licensing — Board for Professional Engineers, Land Surveyors, and Geologists (BPELSG)
*Binding general regulation | No UAS-specific board guidance located*

**Objective Summary:** Business & Professions Code § 8726 defines land surveying to include determining the configuration or contour of the earth's surface, or the position of fixed objects, by applying mathematics or photogrammetry. BPELSG's general rules separately require a licensed PE or PLS to practice only in fields in which they are, by education and/or experience, fully competent and proficient. No standalone BPELSG guidance document specifically addressing UAS-derived photogrammetry, LiDAR, or mapping products was located.

**Practical Interpretation**
- *AEC Industry UAS Expert Perspective:* Because photogrammetric determination of ground configuration/contour falls within the statutory definition of land surveying, route UAS-derived photogrammetry or LiDAR mapping products intended to establish configuration, contour, or fixed-object position through a California-licensed PLS (or PE where applicable).
- *AEC Industry Legal Counsel Perspective:* Confirm whether a given UAS deliverable crosses into the statutory definition of land surveying — that determination controls licensure requirements — and document licensed-professional review in the QA/QC record for any deliverable that does.

### Executive Orders, Court Decisions, and Attorney General Opinions
*Categories reviewed — no UAS-specific state authority located*

**Objective Summary:** No California gubernatorial executive order specifically addressing UAS regulation was located. No California state-court decision directly interpreting a UAS-specific statute was located; a 2024 California Court of Appeal (Fourth District) decision addressed public-records disclosure of police drone footage (not a restriction on private operators) and is noted only as background, not as a source record. No California Attorney General opinion directly involving UAS was located; the AG's office has issued consumer-facing statements on drone registration/safety and joined a multistate comment letter on FAA drone-delivery environmental review, neither of which is a formal AG opinion construing state UAS law.

**Practical Interpretation**
- *AEC Industry UAS Expert Perspective:* No separate executive-order- or AG-opinion-driven requirement to track beyond the statutes and agency policies already covered.
- *AEC Industry Legal Counsel Perspective:* Absence of a located AG opinion or state UAS-specific case is not confirmation none exists; monitor for developments given active legislative activity (e.g., AB 1749) and evolving local-ordinance federal-preemption disputes.

## 4. Unresolved Operational Questions

- **No state preemption of local UAS ordinances:** Unlike Texas, California has not enacted a statute broadly preempting local UAS regulation. California is generally described as a "non-preemption" state in which local governments may adopt their own drone ordinances, subject to an unresolved and evolving line between permissible local regulation of takeoff/landing from local government property and impermissible regulation of airspace/flight operations that may be preempted by FAA field preemption doctrine. A reported 2024 dispute over a Los Angeles municipal drone-filming ordinance (paused by LAPD pending a federal-preemption review after a jury acquittal) illustrates this unresolved boundary. Because local ordinances are deferred to a later research phase, **any specific California project site should be checked against applicable municipal/county drone ordinances before finalizing a flight plan** — this state-level summary does not capture that layer.
- **14 CCR citations for wildlife/state-park provisions:** Several administrative-regulation citations (14 CCR §§ 251, 251.1, 550) were confirmed only via secondary industry-compliance sources rather than independently re-pulled from the Office of Administrative Law's CCR database; recommend direct verification before final client use.
- **Government Code §§ 853–853.5 full text:** Confirmed via secondary aggregation rather than a successful direct fetch of the primary source in this pass; recommend re-verification.
- **BPELSG UAS-specific guidance:** No board-issued UAS-specific guidance was located; this may reflect a genuine regulatory gap rather than a search limitation — a direct inquiry to BPELSG is recommended for novel certification questions.
